# Venice, ca. 1500

Aldus Manutius did for distribution what Gutenberg did for production. His
*Aldine Press*, founded in Venice in 1494, introduced two things still on every
modern bookshelf: italic type, and the octavo — a portable book sized to fit
in a saddlebag or a coat pocket.

The octavo is not a small detail. Folios required a desk; quartos required a
table. Octavos meant a book could travel with its reader. Reading became a
private act, not a civic one.

## What the Aldines published

- Greek and Roman classics, often in scholarly editions
- Vernacular Italian poetry — Petrarch, Dante
- Christian devotionals at lay-reader prices

Aldus paid translators, hired correctors, and put his dolphin-and-anchor mark
on each title page. The mark was a guarantee — not of taste, but of
*authenticity*. A reader could open an Aldine and know, with high confidence,
that no typesetter's quiet improvements had crept in between the manuscript and
the press.

The mark, in other words, was a signature. It bound an edition to a name. It
was the part of the book that could not be forged without the forgery being
visible.

This is older than it looks. Long before public-key cryptography there were
already publishers wrestling with the problem of *which copy do I trust*, and
the answer they reached for was the same one we reach for today: bind the
artifact to an identity, and make tampering with that binding obvious.
