# On Permanence

Books burn. Libraries close. Servers go dark.

The Library of Alexandria did not die in a single fire. It died across
centuries of neglect, civil war, fiscal pressure, and gentle deletion. Most
ancient literature is lost. We know it existed only from references in
surviving works — shadows of shadows.

The first printed books survived because they were *plural*. A medieval
manuscript was an artifact; a printed edition was a population. Burning one
copy meant nothing if a thousand others sat in monasteries and merchant houses
across the continent.

## Three kinds of permanence

1. **Physical.** Acid-free paper, archival ink, a controlled environment.
2. **Cryptographic.** A hash that fails verification if a single bit changes.
3. **Plural.** Many copies, in many places, controlled by many people.

A serious approach needs all three. The first survives time; the second
survives tampering; the third survives politics.

Of the three, the last is the one most often skipped, because it is the one
most often inconvenient. A single canonical host is faster to set up, easier
to monitor, and cheaper to run. It also has a single phone number that can be
called in the middle of the night.

> Centralization is convenience borrowing against availability. The bill comes
> due exactly when you need the system most.

The point of mirrors is not redundancy in the engineering sense. It is
redundancy in the *political* sense: no single party can take a book offline
without the cooperation of every other party that holds a copy. The fewer
parties required to keep a book reachable, the freer the press.
