# Mainz, ca. 1450

Johannes Gutenberg's workshop in Mainz had three things its competitors did not:
oil-based ink that gripped a metal type face, a press adapted from the wooden
screw presses then used for grapes and linen, and — most importantly — a hand
mold that could cast individual letters in a brass matrix to a tolerance of less
than a tenth of a millimeter.

The 42-line Bible was not the first printed book. It was the first book whose
production was *industrial* — repeatable, identical, and decoupled from the
hand that set the type.

What changed in Mainz was less a technology than a relationship. A reader in
Cologne could now hold the same words, set in the same forme, as a reader in
Paris. The book stopped being a *copy* and started being an *edition*.

Two consequences followed almost immediately:

- **Errata.** A mistake in a manuscript was a personal embarrassment; a mistake
  in a printed edition was a public one. The corrigenda — a list of typos
  bound into the back of a book — was effectively invented at this moment.
- **Authority.** When a thousand identical copies could be checked against one
  another, forgery became harder and authorship became more legible.

Gutenberg himself died poor, having lost his press to a creditor in a lawsuit.
The technology outlived him by several centuries and is, in a sense, still
running.
